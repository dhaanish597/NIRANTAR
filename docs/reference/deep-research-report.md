# Indian Government Initiatives  
India has invested heavily in landslide risk mapping and forecasting.  Under the **National Landslide Susceptibility Mapping (NLSM)** program, GSI completed 1:50k mapping of ~4.3 lakh km² in 19 states (Himalayan, NE India, Western Ghats), classifying areas into high/moderate/low hazard.  A landslide inventory (91,000 events, ~33.9k field-validated) was assembled and published via GSI’s portals (NGDR, Bhukosh, etc) and made available on the **Bhusanket** web map and **Bhooskhalan** mobile app.  This data supports regional land‐use planning and local slope zoning.  GSI is now upscaling to meso-scale (1:10k) mapping in 200 “critical sectors” identified from NLSM.  

Concurrently, the Central Government launched the **National Landslide Risk Mitigation Project** (Rs. 1000 Cr for 15 states) and a 2019 Landslide Risk Mitigation Scheme in 4 states.  GSI established a **National Landslide Forecasting Centre (NLFC)** in Kolkata to integrate real-time data (rainfall and soil moisture from IMD, NCMRWF, NRSC) and issue daily forecasts.  Experimental landslide bulletins (daily to seasonal) are already being issued for selected districts: e.g. Darjeeling/Kalimpong (WB), Nilgiris (TN) – now expanding to ~21 districts in 8 states (WB, TN, Uttarakhand, Sikkim, Kerala, Karnataka).  These efforts use probabilistic rainfall‐threshold models (as in the EU LANDSLIP project) in partnership with IMD/NCMRWF/NRSC.  All forecast outputs are disseminated via the Bhusanket portal and Bhooskhalan app.  

Beyond mapping/forecasting, India has invested in **integrated alert systems**.  The CAP‐compliant “SACHET” platform links IMD, CWC, INCOIS, GSI, etc., with State DMAs to send geo‑targeted alerts (SMS/app/browser/RSS/GAGAN/NavIC) in regional languages.  (Over 6400 Cr SMS alerts have been sent so far.)  The state of Assam, for example, has signed an MoU with GSI to pilot a **regional Landslide Early Warning System (LEWS)** in Dima Hasao and Cachar, combining static susceptibility maps with dynamic rainfall forecasts.  Citizens can report cracks or slides via the Bhooskhalan crowdsourcing app to improve situational awareness.  (Similar pilot projects are underway in other hill states.)  

# International Early Warning Systems  
Worldwide there are a few established landslide EWS examples.  Hong Kong’s **Landslip Warning System** – one of the oldest – uses a dense network of 120+ automated rain gauges and terrain‐model data to issue “Landslip Warnings” (for widespread heavy rain) or localized “Special Landslip Advisories”.  Warnings are broadcast on TV/radio and posted on government apps and social media.  In Norway, a national system (NVE/MET/NPRA) issues daily landslide-hazard bulletins using hydrological models that combine precipitation, snowmelt, groundwater levels and susceptibility maps.  Over 800 warnings (mostly “yellow” level) have been issued in the last decade, and forecasts are available on the public **Varsom.no** portal and app.  Other countries (Taiwan, Italy, Brazil, Indonesia, Scotland, etc.) have regional or project-based warning schemes; for example, Italy’s **SANF** system uses empirically‐calibrated rainfall thresholds on gauge data to trigger alerts.  The international network *LandAware* (200+ experts from 46 countries) notes that many systems vary widely in maturity; Switzerland, for instance, plans a new national slope-instability warning system by 2025.  

Global-scale research projects also exist.  NASA’s **LHASA** (Landslide Hazard Analysis for Situational Awareness) uses real-time precipitation and ML models to produce global daily landslide‐hazard maps (~1 km resolution), updated several times per day.  Public hazard layers and exposure assessments (population/roads) can be accessed via NASA’s ArcGIS services.  In the remote-sensing domain, competitions like **Landslide4Sense (IARAI 2022)** have assembled annotated multi-sensor satellite datasets from landslides worldwide and spurred development of automated detection algorithms.  For example, open-source models (U-Net variants) have been trained on global landslide imagery (RGB+NIR+DEM) to map landslide scars after events.  Similarly, NASA’s SALaD toolkit applies object-based image analysis and random forests to map landslides from optical images.  Even hackathons (e.g. a Hong Kong “landslide prevention” challenge on Zindi) have produced ML models that classify terrain-feature grids as slide/no-slide.  On-the-ground IoT prototypes (tiltmeters, moisture/vibration sensors) combined with ML have been trialed to catch small slope movements.  

# Summary of Gaps and Proposed Features  
Current solutions excel at *forecasting* (rainfall‐trigger thresholds, modeling) and *post-event mapping*, but largely **stop short of integrated response**.  For example, published EWS focus on issuing warnings (often through apps or sirens).  They do not generally include **dynamic evacuation routing**, real-time critical-infrastructure status, or decision-support for officials.  Likewise, citizen reporting is just beginning to be tapped (e.g. Bhooskhalan’s crowdsourcing).  Many systems also assume continuous connectivity, which is unrealistic in remote NE India.  

Our solution can add novel capabilities beyond prediction.  For example, **dynamic evacuation routing** (via GIS network analysis) can direct people along safe paths avoiding known hazards or blocked roads – a feature not offered in existing EWS.  A **“time-to-event” countdown** could be synthesized from nowcasting models (e.g. when rainfall thresholds will be exceeded), giving actionable lead time if the model confidence is high.  Mapping **critical infrastructure** (hospitals, shelters, bridges) on the same dashboard would help priorities – a step beyond typical hazard heatmaps.  We propose an **AI “Emergency Commander”** – essentially an expert system or chatbot – to advise officials on resource allocation (e.g. deploy patrols, pre-position relief supplies) based on the alert level and location.  Such interactive decision support is largely missing in current practice.  

Our plan also addresses logistic gaps noted by Indian authorities: offline functionality, multi-language alerts, community volunteers, etc.  India’s CAP/SACHET already ensures geo‐targeted warnings in local languages via SMS/apps.  We will build on this by supporting vernacular alerts and fallback to SMS/voice for low-bandwidth areas.  **Offline sync** (with periodic mobile-network bursts) is essential for NER’s remote zones.  **Crowd-sourced inputs** (crack photos, road blockages) will be integrated via the app (like current Bhooskhalan), improving situational awareness.  Finally, explainable **risk scores** can be computed for each location (leveraging our ML model): not just “high risk”, but with feature-level reasons (rainfall + soil moisture + slope angle) to help users trust the warning.  

In summary, while existing Indian and international systems provide robust data collection and warning issuance, there is ample room to augment them with response and planning tools.  By integrating routing, simulation (“what-if”), offline operation, and a multi-lingual alert/communication layer, our platform would fill gaps left by current solutions.  All findings above (from government plans to global research) underscore the timeliness of our approach and suggest best practices we will adopt.
